Cost Basis Tracking for a Florida Primary Residence

A plan to reconstruct historical cost basis from receipts scattered across email, cloud storage, and local files — and to capture every qualifying expense from here forward, in a form that survives an IRS challenge decades from now.

  • Prepared for Sam
  • Property type Primary residence
  • State Florida
  • Status Awaiting input on open questions
  • Version 0.1
01

Why this is worth doing

When you eventually sell, your federal taxable gain is sale price − selling expenses − adjusted basis. Section 121 lets you exclude $250,000 of that gain if you file single, or $500,000 filing jointly. Those limits were set in 1997 and have never been indexed to inflation, so in much of Florida a long-held home now blows straight through them.

Adjusted basis is the only lever you still control after purchase. Every qualifying improvement you can document raises basis dollar-for-dollar and lowers the gain dollar-for-dollar. Undocumented improvements are worth nothing — the burden of proof is on the taxpayer, and a memory of "we redid the kitchen around 2016" is not proof.

Florida has no state income tax, so this is a purely federal exercise. Any gain above the exclusion is taxed at long-term capital gains rates, potentially plus the 3.8% net investment income tax. A well-kept basis file is realistically worth tens of thousands of dollars at sale, and the cost of keeping it is a few hours a quarter.

Not tax advice

This plan encodes the general rules in IRS Publication 523 and Publication 551 so that you collect the right evidence. It is a record-keeping system, not a tax opinion. Every judgment call about whether a specific expense is a capital improvement should be confirmed with your CPA before it lands on a return.

02

What actually counts — the rules the system must encode

The design goal is that the tool mirrors the structure of IRS Publication 523, Worksheet 2. If the data model matches the worksheet, then producing a defensible number at sale is a report, not a research project.

Items that increase basis

Worksheet 2 lineBucketWhat belongs here
4aPurchase priceWhat you paid, including down payment and the full amount borrowed. If you built, the cost of the land plus construction cost.
4bAcquisition closing costsAbstract/title search fees, owner's title insurance, recording fees, survey fees, transfer and stamp taxes, legal fees, utility installation charges. Excludes anything financing-related.
4cSeller costs you absorbedSeller's back taxes, seller's recording fees, seller-responsibility repairs you agreed to pay and were never reimbursed for.
4dCapital improvementsThe big one. Work that adds value, prolongs useful life, or adapts the home to a new use — and that is still part of the home at sale.
4eDamage repairAmounts spent repairing damage to the home or the land it sits on. Highly relevant after a Florida named storm.
4fSpecial assessmentsHOA or municipal assessments for local improvements — not assessments that are merely for repairs or ongoing maintenance.

  • Additions: bedroom, bathroom, deck, garage, porch, patio
  • Lawn & grounds: landscaping, driveway, walkway, fence, retaining wall, swimming pool
  • Systems: heating, central A/C, furnace, ductwork, humidifier, central vacuum, filtration, wiring, security system, lawn sprinklers

  • Insulation: attic, walls, floors, pipes and ductwork
  • Plumbing: septic system, water heater, soft water system, filtration
  • Interior: built-in appliances, kitchen modernization, flooring, wall-to-wall carpeting, fireplace

Items that decrease basis — the part most homeowners forget

Tracking only the additions produces an overstated basis, which is the failure mode that actually gets penalized. The system must give equal weight to reductions.

Worksheet 2 lineReductionFlorida relevance
5aDepreciation taken or allowable for business or rental useHome office deductions, a period as a rental, or short-term rental use. Note "or allowable" — it bites even if you never claimed it.
5bCasualty losses claimed as a federal deductionHurricane and flood loss deductions taken in a federally declared disaster year.
5cInsurance payments received or expected for casualty lossesWindstorm, flood, and Citizens claim proceeds. Applies even while a claim is still open.
5dPayments for granting an easement or right-of-wayUtility, drainage, or conservation easements.

5f–5lAdoption credits, seller-paid taxes and points, forgiven mortgage debt, deducted sales tax, builder-provided temporary housing, pre-1997 postponed gainSituational, but cheap to screen for once.

What is explicitly excluded

  • Repairs and maintenance that merely keep the home in good condition — painting, fixing leaks, patching cracks, replacing broken hardware.
  • Improvements no longer part of the home. Carpet you installed in 2012 and tore out in 2021 comes back out of basis. This is why the data model needs a "superseded by" link, not just an append-only list.
  • Anything with a useful life under one year when installed.
  • Financing costs — points, origination fees, appraisal required by the lender, credit report fees, mortgage insurance, refinance costs.
  • Your own labor. Sweat equity has zero basis. Materials you bought for a DIY project do count.
The exception worth knowing

Repair-type work counts when it is part of an extensive remodel or restoration. Replacing one broken windowpane is a repair; replacing that same window as part of replacing all the windows is an improvement. Likewise, post-casualty repairs that restore the home to its pre-casualty condition increase basis — offset by any insurance reimbursement received or expected. The practical consequence: capture whole projects, not loose receipts. A receipt-level tool that cannot group line items into a project will misclassify a large share of Florida storm work.

03

Florida-specific considerations

Hurricane hardening and mitigation

Impact windows and doors, roof-to-wall straps, secondary water barriers, reinforced garage doors, and whole-house generators are classic basis-increasing improvements. But if you took a My Safe Florida Home matching grant (state pays $2 per $1 up to $10,000), only your out-of-pocket share belongs in basis — the grant portion is a subsidy, not your cost. Same logic for FEMA mitigation grants and utility rebates.

Storm damage and insurance

Named-storm repairs increase basis under line 4e, but insurance proceeds and any deducted casualty loss reduce it. For every storm event the system needs a linked record: repair spend, claim number, proceeds received, proceeds still expected, and whether a casualty loss was deducted. Getting this pairing wrong in either direction is the single largest accuracy risk in a Florida basis file.

Closing costs and doc stamps

Florida charges documentary stamp tax on the deed at $0.70 per $100 of price ($0.60 in Miami-Dade), customarily paid by the seller, plus $0.35 per $100 on the note and $0.20 per $100 intangible tax on the mortgage, customarily paid by the buyer. Deed stamps you paid go into basis; the mortgage-side taxes are financing costs and do not. Your actual settlement statement governs, not the custom — so the closing document itself must be in the vault.

Coastal and lot improvements

Seawalls, docks, boat lifts, screen enclosures and lanais, pools, pavers, irrigation wells, septic-to-sewer conversion, and elevation work are all basis-eligible and common here. Many require permits, which is convenient: a permit record is durable third-party evidence that survives long after a contractor goes out of business.

Property tax exemptions are a separate matter

Homestead exemption, Save Our Homes assessment caps, and portability affect your annual property tax bill. They have no effect on federal cost basis. Keep them out of this system so the basis number stays clean; note them elsewhere if useful.

Public records as a fallback source

Florida county building departments and property appraisers publish permit and improvement history online. Where receipts are permanently lost, a permit record plus a bank or card statement showing payment to the permitted contractor is a far stronger reconstruction than an estimate. Treat county records as a first-class harvest source, not a last resort.

Recommendation

Split the problem. Harvesting decades of scattered receipts is a one-time batch job that genuinely needs automation. Recording a handful of new projects a year is a data-entry job that does not. So: build a disposable ingestion pipeline for the historical sweep, and land its output in a durable, boring system of record — a spreadsheet plus an organized document vault — that will still open without any special software in 2045.

Only promote the system of record to a real application if the open questions reveal high volume (say, more than ~150 historical items), multiple people entering data, or a genuine need for automated reconciliation against bank feeds.

05

Architecture

Five components. The document vault and the ledger are permanent; everything else is replaceable machinery pointed at them.

ComponentResponsibilityImplementation
1. Document vault Immutable archive of every source document. Never edited, only added to. This is the evidence. A plain folder tree, backed up in three places. Files named YYYY-MM-DD__vendor__short-description__amount.pdf. Content-hashed so duplicates from multiple sources collapse.
2. Ledger One row per basis event, each pointing at its documents. The system of record. Spreadsheet (or a table in the app). Columns map one-to-one onto Pub 523 Worksheet 2 lines.
3. Harvest connectors Pull candidate documents out of email, cloud storage, local disk, and financial statements into a staging area. Read-only API access or bulk export per source. Run once for history, then on a schedule or on demand.
4. Extraction and classification Turn a document into a proposed ledger row: date, vendor, amount, category, capital-vs-repair call, confidence. OCR for scans and photos, plus a rules layer for known vendors and an LLM assist for the ambiguous middle. Never auto-commits.
5. Review and reporting Human approves or rejects every proposed row; produces the basis schedule and gain estimate. A queue plus a small set of reports. The approval step is what makes the output trustworthy.

Sequence this correctly

Do the financial statement sweep first, even though it yields the weakest evidence. It produces the definitive list of what projects exist and what they cost. Everything after that becomes a targeted hunt for documents you know are out there, rather than an open-ended trawl through twenty years of email hoping something turns up.

07

Extraction, classification, and review

Every harvested document runs the same path. Nothing reaches the ledger without a human approving it.

  1. Ingest and hash. Copy into the vault, compute a content hash, drop exact duplicates, keep a record of every source that produced the file.
  2. Normalize. Convert to PDF or PNG. OCR anything that is an image or a scan.
  3. Extract fields. Vendor, date, total, line items, invoice number, payment method, permit number.
  4. Classify. A deterministic rules layer first (known vendors, known keywords, amount thresholds), then an LLM for the residue, returning a proposed category, worksheet line, and treatment along with a confidence score and a one-line rationale.
  5. Group into projects. Cluster by vendor, date proximity, and description so the remodel/restoration exception can be applied to whole jobs.
  6. Queue for review. Sort by dollar impact descending — your attention should go where the money is.
  7. Approve, edit, or reject. Approval writes the ledger row. Rejection is retained with a reason, so the same document is not reconsidered on every future run.
  8. Flag for CPA. A dedicated status for genuinely ambiguous calls, exported as a single list at year end rather than as a trickle of one-off questions.
Design constraint

Automated classification must never write directly to the ledger. The value of this system at audit time is that a human reviewed and stands behind every line. An LLM's guess about whether a $9,400 payment was a re-roof or a roof repair is a starting point for your judgment, not a substitute for it.

08

Capturing new expenses going forward

The historical sweep is a project. This is the part that has to still be working in ten years, which means it has to be nearly effortless. Three capture paths, all landing in the same review queue:

Forward-to-file

A dedicated email address for the property. Forward any receipt to it and it is auto-ingested. Give the address to contractors as the billing contact so their invoices arrive there directly.

Snap-and-drop

Phone photo of a paper receipt into a watched cloud folder. OCR and extraction happen on ingest; you confirm later at a desk rather than in a parking lot.

Direct entry

A short form for the case where you know the answer immediately — especially useful at the moment a project completes, when the scope and the reasoning are still fresh.

Standing habits worth adopting

  • Open a project record before work starts, not after. Attach the bid, the contract, the permit, and before-photos as they arrive.
  • Pay by card, check, or transfer. Cash payments to contractors are nearly impossible to substantiate later. If cash is unavoidable, get a signed paid-in-full receipt and photograph the work.
  • Photograph before and after, with the phone's location and timestamp on. Costs nothing, corroborates everything.
  • Record the reasoning for any capital-vs-repair judgment at the time you make it. Reconstructing your own logic years later is surprisingly hard.
  • Log removals. When something is torn out, mark the original event still_in_home = false immediately. This is the discipline that keeps the number honest.
  • Quarterly review, annual close. Clear the queue every quarter; each January, reconcile against the prior year's card statements to catch anything missed, and archive a snapshot.

Security, privacy, and retention

Access
  • Read-only, narrowly scoped credentials for every connector. Nothing needs write access to your email.
  • Revoke connector access as soon as the historical sweep completes; re-grant only if you re-run it.
  • Keep API keys out of the repository and out of the spreadsheet.
Data handling
  • Documents contain account numbers, signatures, and addresses. Prefer local processing.
  • If an LLM is used for classification, decide deliberately whether full documents may leave the machine or only redacted extracted fields.
  • Encrypt the vault at rest; encrypt any backup that leaves the house.
Retention
  • Basis records must survive until three years after the return reporting the sale — potentially decades from now.
  • Seven years post-sale is the safer target given substantial-understatement lookbacks.
  • Keep originals, not just extracted data. The PDF is the evidence; the spreadsheet row is only an index.
Durability
  • Three copies, two media types, one off-site. Verify a restore annually — an untested backup is a rumor.
  • Open formats only: PDF, PNG, CSV. No proprietary container that needs a live subscription to read.
  • Make sure a spouse or executor knows where the vault is and how to open it.

Phased roadmap

Sequenced so that value lands early and the expensive automation is only built if the earlier phases prove it is warranted. Effort labels describe scope and complexity, not calendar time.

Phase 0 — Resolve the open questions

Low effort

Answer the questions in the second tab. Several of them — volume of history, prior rental or home-office use, whether the closing statement survives — materially change the design.

Done when: the questionnaire is answered and the build-vs-spreadsheet call in section 04 can be made on evidence rather than assumption.

Phase 1 — Anchor the starting basis

Low effort

Locate the acquisition documents: closing disclosure or HUD-1/ALTA settlement statement, deed, and the original survey. Extract lines 4a, 4b, and 4c. Request archived copies from the title company or closing attorney now if they are missing, since retention windows are finite.

Done when: purchase price and includible closing costs are recorded with the settlement statement in the vault.

Phase 2 — Stand up the vault and ledger

Low effort

Create the folder structure, naming convention, backup routine, and the ledger with the schema from section 05, pre-populated with the Pub 523 categories and worksheet lines as dropdowns. Enter the handful of projects you already remember, to validate the schema against reality.

Done when: a working ledger exists, backed up in three places, and produces a basis subtotal.

Phase 3 — Financial sweep and project inventory

Medium effort

Export every year of bank and card statements available. Identify large and home-improvement-coded transactions. Build the master list of projects with dates, vendors, and amounts — independent of whether receipts have been found yet.

Done when: a project inventory exists spanning the full ownership period, each entry marked as documented or needing evidence.

Phase 4 — Document harvest

High effort

Run the email, cloud, local, photo, retailer, and county-records sweeps against the project inventory. Deduplicate by hash into the vault. This is the phase where automation pays for itself, and where the disposable ingestion pipeline gets built if volume justifies it.

Done when: every project in the inventory is either documented, reconstructed from permit plus payment evidence, or explicitly written off as unrecoverable.

Phase 5 — Classification and reconciliation

Medium effort

Work the review queue largest-dollar-first. Apply the reductions: depreciation, casualty losses deducted, insurance proceeds, energy credits, mitigation grants, easement payments. Back out improvements that are no longer part of the home. Flag the genuinely ambiguous for your CPA.

Done when: the ledger produces an adjusted basis figure with every row carrying a document reference and a confidence rating.

Phase 6 — Ongoing capture

Low effort

Turn on the forwarding address, the watched folder, and the quick-entry form. Put the quarterly review and January reconciliation on the calendar as recurring commitments.

Done when: a new receipt reaches the ledger with under a minute of your attention, and the review cadence is scheduled.

Phase 7 — Validate and hand off

Low effort

Have your CPA review the schedule and the flagged items once, while the reasoning is fresh and any gaps are still fixable. Perform a full restore test from backup. Document where everything lives for a spouse or executor.

Done when: a third party has confirmed the schedule is usable and a restore from cold backup has succeeded.

Conventions worth fixing early

  • File naming: YYYY-MM-DD__vendor__description__amount.pdf. Date-first sorts chronologically everywhere, forever.
  • Folder layout: /vault/<year>/<project-id>/, with acquisition documents in /vault/00-acquisition/.
  • Never edit a vault file. Corrections go in the ledger's notes, not in the evidence.
  • One amount convention: record your net out-of-pocket cost, and put any offsetting grant, rebate, or insurance payment in reimbursement as a separate visible number rather than silently netting it.
